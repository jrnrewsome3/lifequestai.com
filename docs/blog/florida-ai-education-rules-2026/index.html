Florida's New AI Education Rules: What Families and Teachers Need to Know

Florida now has binding AI policies for schools and public colleges. Learn what the rules mean for parents, privacy, classroom tools, and human judgment.

LifeQuest AI
November 11, 2026 · 4 min read

Florida has moved beyond general advice about artificial intelligence in education. On September 16, 2026, the State Board of Education approved rules governing AI use in district and charter schools and in Florida College System institutions. The aim is not to keep technology out of education. It is to make schools explain how AI is used, protect students, and keep people responsible for teaching and learning.

For parents, teachers, and adult learners, the practical question is simple: Who controls the tool, what information does it collect, and does it actually help someone learn?

What changed—and when?

Florida adopted two relevant rules with different scopes. The amended Internet Safety Policy rule (6A-1.0957) took effect on October 29, 2026. It directs district school boards and charter school governing boards to update their AI instructional-tool safety policies by July 1, 2027. A separate Florida College System rule (6A-14.0719) took effect on October 20, 2026 and addresses institutional AI policies.

That distinction matters. The September announcement did not mean that every district had already implemented every safeguard that day. And requirements written for parents of schoolchildren should not automatically be presented as identical requirements for adult college students or every university in Florida.

What parents should expect from K–12 schools

Under the school rule, districts and charter school governing boards must establish policies addressing these protections:

  • Clear notice. Families must be told when an AI instructional tool is approved, including its name, grades and subjects, and the kind of interaction students will have with it.
  • A meaningful choice. When a student would directly use the tool, parents must have an option to opt in and information about a substantively comparable non-AI alternative.
  • Extra scrutiny for younger children. AI instructional tools proposed for voluntary prekindergarten through grade 5 receive additional developmental and age-appropriateness review.
  • Limits on companion-style systems. Instructional tools may not be designed to simulate friendship, meet students' social or emotional needs, or use humanlike relationship-building features to keep children engaged with a machine.
  • Student-data protections. Policies restrict selling student information, using it to train agentic models, and undisclosed monitoring, psychological profiling, or social scoring.
  • Academic integrity and security. The rules call for expectations around responsible academic work and incident reporting when an AI agent compromises district data-system integrity.

These are obligations for institutions and vendors, not a promise that every product on a classroom screen is automatically safe. Families can still ask what a specific tool does, who can see its output, and how its usefulness will be evaluated.

Teachers remain responsible for instruction

The Department of Education describes AI as additional support for teachers, not a substitute for them. That is a practical standard worth defending. An AI tutor might offer another explanation of fractions or help a student practice a language. A teacher still needs to judge whether the explanation is correct, whether the student understands it, and whether the activity builds independent thinking.

State education leaders also directed updates to K–12 computer science standards and the Florida Digital Classrooms Plan. The announced direction emphasizes instructional results, evidence of effectiveness, responsible technology use, and professional learning for educators—not screen time alone. Those directions should be distinguished from changes already completed.

What about Florida colleges?

Florida College System institutions are covered by a separate AI-policy rule. Colleges must address how AI is governed in their own educational settings. The school-specific parental opt-in provision should not be described as a blanket rule for college adults. Students should check their institution's policies on approved tools, academic integrity, privacy, and use of AI in coursework.

Five questions worth asking now

  1. Which AI tools are approved for my child's course or for this college program?
  2. Does the student use the system directly, and what non-AI option is available where required?
  3. What information is collected, retained, or shared with the vendor?
  4. How does a teacher check whether AI assistance improved learning rather than simply completed the assignment?
  5. Where can students and families find the policy, report a problem, or request clarification?

One useful family exercise is to ask an approved AI tool to explain a familiar topic, then compare its answer with a textbook or another trusted source. Identify one helpful part, one omission, and one question that still requires a person. Do not enter student records or sensitive personal information into an unapproved service.

Responsible AI is a skill, not just a rule

Florida's action is important because it puts boundaries around AI use while recognizing that students will need AI literacy in work and life. Policies can create guardrails. They cannot replace the everyday habit of checking answers, protecting personal information, and deciding when not to use a tool.

That is the approach we teach at LifeQuest AI: start with a real task, use the technology deliberately, verify what it produces, and keep human judgment at the center. The goal is not to raise a generation that never touches AI. It is to help people become capable of using it without surrendering their thinking.
